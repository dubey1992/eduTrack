A school timetable is a promise made to everyone at once. Each class knows what it has next, each teacher knows where to be, and the bells ring at the right times. It only takes one mistake, such as a teacher booked into two classes in the same period, for the morning to unravel.

Most of those mistakes can be prevented when the timetable is built, rather than discovered on the day.

## Start with the bells, not the subjects

Before placing a single lesson, set out the day itself.

- **Periods.** When each one starts and ends. A period is a slot in the day, not a subject.
- **Breaks.** Short break, lunch, assembly. Many schools have more than one break a day, and they belong in the plan as much as the lessons do, so the day reads as the school actually rings it.
- **Half days.** A shorter day usually has its own bells: fewer periods, different times. Treat it as its own shape of day, rather than a full day with lessons crossed out.
- **The school week.** Which days the school opens, and which of them are half days.

Once the frame is right, the lessons fit into it, and every screen that shows "now" or "next" has the right answer.

## One rule that prevents most clashes

The most damaging clash is a teacher placed in two class sections in the same period on the same day. One class ends up without a teacher, and the problem often surfaces only when a student comes looking.

A timetable should refuse that booking at the moment it is made, and say which class the teacher is already with. It is far easier to move a lesson while building the grid than to cover a missing teacher on a Monday morning.

The same goes for each class's own grid: one subject in each period, never two.

## Build class by class, then check by teacher

Fill each class's week first, subject by subject, keeping an eye on how many periods each subject gets. Then look at the week from each teacher's side.

- Is anyone teaching six periods in a row with no break?
- Does a teacher who is also a head of department have time left for that work?
- Are the hardest subjects all after lunch for one class?

A timetable that is fair to teachers is one that lasts the year without constant swaps.

## Make the timetable do more than hang on a wall

A timetable becomes far more useful when other parts of the school day read from it. A teacher's phone can show today's periods in order, with the current one marked, and a daily teaching report can be filed against the period that was actually taught.
