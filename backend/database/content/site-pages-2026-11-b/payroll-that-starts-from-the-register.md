In many schools, payroll week starts the same way: someone totals the staff attendance register by hand, checks it against the leave book, and types the result into a spreadsheet of salaries. It takes days, and every mistake is somebody's pay.

Most of that work disappears when attendance, leave and salaries live in one place, and payroll simply reads the register.

## Agree the rules before the first run

Payroll arguments are rarely about arithmetic. They are about rules nobody wrote down. Settle these once, and tell staff.

- **What a working day is.** The school's own week, minus its holidays. A Saturday the school is closed is not a day anyone missed.
- **What is paid.** A day present is paid, and so is a day of approved leave. A half day is half paid. An absence is unpaid.
- **What happens when nobody marked the register.** A day with no mark should count as paid. Staff should never lose money because the office forgot a page.
- **What is pro-rated.** If pay goes down for unpaid days, decide whether allowances and deductions go down with it. Pro-rating all of them in the same way is the easiest rule to explain.

With those rules in place, a month's pay is one sum: monthly pay multiplied by paid days, divided by working days.

## Keep salaries simple and visible

A salary is easier to check when it is broken into a few named parts: the basic pay, the allowances the school gives, and the deductions it takes. Each one is a fixed amount the school sets for that person. When somebody asks why their pay changed, the answer is on one line, not in a formula.

## Run the month in three steps

A good payroll run has a point where nothing can change any more, and a record of when the money left.

1. **Draft.** The month is worked out from the register. The office reviews it and makes any adjustments, such as a one-off bonus or a correction.
2. **Finalize.** The payslips are locked. From here, what each person was told they would be paid cannot quietly change.
3. **Mark as paid.** Record the date and how the money was sent, whether by bank transfer, cheque or cash, so the books and the payslips agree.

## Let staff see their own payslips

Most payroll questions come from people who cannot see how their pay was worked out. A payslip that shows the paid days, the working days and each part of the salary answers most of them before they are asked. Sending it by email and showing it on the staff member's phone answers the rest.

## Give payroll its own role

Payroll is sensitive. The person who runs it needs to see every salary, but nothing else in the school's records requires that, and most staff should see only their own. A separate role for whoever runs payroll keeps salaries with the people who need them.
