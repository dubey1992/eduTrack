A poor report card rarely comes out of nowhere. In most cases the signs were there for weeks: a few more absences than usual, a test mark that dropped, then another. The difficulty is not that the signs are hidden. It is that they are spread across registers, mark sheets and different teachers, and nobody sees them side by side until the term is over.

This post looks at which early signs matter most, and how a school can act on them while there is still time.

## The two signs that matter most

Schools collect a great deal of information about each student. Two pieces of it are especially good at predicting trouble.

- **Attendance.** A student who misses lessons misses teaching, and the gap grows quietly. A child who drops from almost every day to three or four days a week is often struggling with something, at school or at home.
- **Test results.** Class tests and assignments show progress far earlier than end-of-term exams. A low average is a warning. A falling one, even from a good start, is often the earlier and more useful signal.

Neither sign needs anything new to be recorded. Teachers already take the register and already mark tests. What is missing is someone, or something, looking at both together and saying "this child needs a conversation this week".

## Set clear lines, not gut feelings

An early warning only helps if everybody agrees what it means. Decide, as a school, the lines that put a student on the list. For example:

- attendance below 75% over the last 30 school days;
- a test average below the pass mark over the last 90 days;
- a fall of ten points or more in either, compared with the period before.

Clear lines make the list fair. A student is on it because of the numbers, not because of a reputation, and a teacher can explain exactly why when they speak to a family.

### One reason or two

A student who crosses one line may just be having a difficult month. A student who crosses two, say low attendance and falling marks together, usually needs help sooner. Separating the two gives the school a simple order of priority: start with the students who are struggling on more than one front.

## Put the list in front of the right people

A list that only the principal sees changes little. The people who can act are the class teacher, who knows the student, and the head of department, who can see whether a subject is the problem rather than the child. Each should see the students they are responsible for, with the reason written out in plain words, not a score.

## Turn a name on a list into a plan

The value is in what happens next. A short, routine response works better than a dramatic one:

- the class teacher has a quiet word with the student, the same week;
- if attendance is the issue, the family hears from the school before it becomes a habit;
- if one subject is the issue, the subject teacher looks at which topics went wrong and offers practice on those;
- the student comes off the list when the numbers recover, which is itself worth telling them.

Checking the list once a week is enough. The point is to notice in week four, not week fourteen.
