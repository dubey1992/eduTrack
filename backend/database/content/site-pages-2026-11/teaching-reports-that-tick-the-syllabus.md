Most schools ask teachers to record what they taught, and most teachers find it a chore. The report is written in one place, the syllabus tracker is updated in another, and by Friday nobody quite remembers which topic was covered on Tuesday. Heads of department end the term unsure how far each class really got.

It does not have to be like that. A teaching report can be short, filed in a minute, and do two jobs at once.

## Why record teaching at all

A daily teaching report answers questions that otherwise come up too late:

- **Is each class on track?** If a syllabus has forty topics and a term has ten weeks, a class that has covered eight topics by week five needs attention now, not in the last fortnight.
- **What did a class miss?** When a teacher is away or a period is lost to an event, the record shows exactly which topics need catching up.
- **Is a struggling class being taught the topics it is tested on?** Comparing what was taught with test results often explains a weak subject quickly.

## Make the report quick

Teachers will file a report that takes a minute. They will put off one that takes ten. A few habits keep it short.

### One report per period taught

Tie each report to a period in the timetable. The teacher does not have to type the class, the subject or the time: they are already known. What is left is what happened in the lesson.

### Pick the topic, do not type it

If the syllabus is already listed for the class, the teacher picks the topic taught from that list. Picking is faster than typing, it is spelt the same way every time, and it means the report can be matched against the syllabus without anyone reading it.

### Allow it on the phone

A teacher walking out of a classroom has their phone, not a desk. A report that can be filed from the phone gets filed the same day.

## Let the report keep the syllabus up to date

This is the part that saves the most time. When a teacher picks the topic they taught, that topic can be marked as covered for the class there and then. There is no second form and no end-of-week catch-up: the syllabus progress is simply the sum of the reports.

Keep the syllabus per class rather than per section. Class 7 A and Class 7 B study the same chapters, so they should share one list, each with its own progress.

## Give heads of department a reason to read them

Reports that nobody reads stop being written. A head of department should be able to review their department's reports, accept them, or send one back with a note when something needs correcting. A teacher who gets a short "thanks" or a useful question back is far more likely to keep filing.

### Be fair about the gaps

Not every missing report is a lapse. A teacher on approved leave should not be chased for the periods they were away, and a holiday should not count as a missing day. Getting this right is what makes the list of missing reports something teachers trust rather than resent.
