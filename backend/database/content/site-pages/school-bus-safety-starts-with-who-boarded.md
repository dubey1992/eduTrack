When something goes wrong on a school bus, the first question anyone asks is simple: who was on board? This post looks at why an accurate record of who boarded and who got off is the foundation of bus safety, and how schools can keep one.

## The question that comes first

Schools rightly put effort into vehicles, drivers and routes. Buses are inspected, drivers are checked, speed limits are enforced. All of that matters. But in an accident, a breakdown, or a call from a worried parent, the very first thing the school needs is a list of the children on that bus at that moment.

Many schools cannot produce that list quickly. They know which students are assigned to a route, but not who actually got on today. A child who was off sick, collected by a relative, or put on the wrong bus does not show up in an assignment list.

## Assignment is not attendance

A route list says who should be on the bus. A boarding record says who is. The difference is where most bus incidents start:

- a child who never reached the stop, and whose parents believe they are at school;
- a child who got off at the wrong stop, or not at all;
- a young child who fell asleep and was still on the bus at the depot.

Each of these is caught by one habit: marking every child on and off the bus, every trip.

## What a good boarding record looks like

A useful record is simple enough for crew to keep in a moving vehicle, in all weather, often with a phone in one hand.

### Every child, every stop

At each stop, the attendant or driver marks each expected child as boarded, or absent if they do not come. On the way home, each child is marked as dropped when they get off. A child cannot be dropped who was never boarded, which makes mistakes easier to spot.

### A time on every mark

A mark is far more useful with the time it happened. If a parent calls at 7:40 asking whether their child has been picked up, the office can answer straight away.

### Works without signal

Rural roads and city underpasses lose signal. A record that only works online will have gaps exactly where they are most likely to matter. Marks should be saved on the phone and sent as soon as signal returns.

### An end-of-trip check

Before the trip is closed, every boarded child should be accounted for. On the way home, the bus should not finish its trip with a child still marked as on board. A simple rule helps here: the crew walk the bus, front to back, before ending the trip.

## Telling families

Once boarding is recorded, families can be told. A short message when a child boards and when they are dropped off reassures parents, and a message when a child does not board gives them a chance to say why. Like any alert, these work best when the wording is short and the contact numbers are accurate.

## When something goes wrong

The crew need a fast way to raise the alarm, one that works with one hand and does not depend on finding a contact. The school office needs to know which bus it is, where it is, and who is on board. With an accurate boarding record, the office can start calling the right families within minutes, instead of guessing from a route list.

## Habits that keep the record honest

- Train every driver and attendant on the same routine, and check it is being followed.
- Keep route assignments up to date when families move or change their plans.
- Review trips that ended with children unaccounted for, even if it turned out to be a marking slip.
- Treat a missing mark as a question to answer, not a gap to ignore.
