When a student does not arrive at school, the most useful thing a family can learn is that fact, early. This post looks at why same-morning absence alerts matter, and what makes them work in practice.

## The gap between the register and the phone call

In many schools, an absence is noticed at registration and then waits. It sits on a paper register until someone in the office has time to ring round, or it shows up days later in a report. By then the question "where was my child on Tuesday morning?" is a much harder one to answer.

Most absences have an ordinary reason. A child is unwell and a parent forgot to call, or a dentist appointment was never passed on. But a few do not. A student who left home in uniform and never reached the gate is exactly the case where a parent needs to know within the hour, not at the end of the week.

A same-morning alert closes that gap. The register is taken, and the families of absent students hear about it while there is still time to act.

## What families do with the message

An alert does three useful things.

- It confirms the expected. Most parents of a sick child simply read it and carry on. It costs them nothing and reassures them the school is paying attention.
- It catches the unexpected. A parent who believes their child is in class finds out quickly that they are not, and can start making calls straight away.
- It corrects the record. A parent who replies that their child is on a family trip, or arrived late, helps the office fix the register the same day rather than chasing it later.

Over time, alerts also change behaviour. When students know their families hear about an absence that morning, skipping the first lesson becomes much less appealing.

## Making alerts work, not just exist

Sending a text is easy. Sending one that families trust and act on takes a little care.

### Take the register early and once

An alert can only go out once the register is in. If registers trickle in across the morning, so do the alerts, and families lose the benefit of hearing early. Agree a time by which every class register is taken, and make it easy for teachers to meet it, including in classrooms with poor signal.

### Get the contact numbers right

An alert sent to an old number helps nobody. Check guardian mobile numbers at admission and again at the start of each year. A short list of students with no number on record is one of the most useful things an office can work through.

### Alert on absence, not on everything

It is tempting to tell parents about every arrival too. In practice, a message every morning saying a child turned up quickly becomes noise that parents learn to ignore. Keeping alerts for absences means the one message that matters still gets read.

### Keep the wording plain

The best alert says who, what and when in one or two short sentences, with the school's name and a number to call. Avoid anything that reads like an accusation. A parent whose child is ill does not want to feel told off.

### Be careful with corrections

Mistakes happen: a student marked absent was in the library, or arrived ten minutes late. Decide in advance how the office will handle a correction, and whether it calls the family. A quick follow-up call does more for trust than a second automated message.

## A small change with a large effect

Same-morning absence alerts are not complicated. They depend on three habits most schools already have the pieces for: a register taken early, accurate contact details, and a clear, kind message. Put together, they turn a missing child from a line in a report into a phone call that same morning.
